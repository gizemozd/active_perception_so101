XM and XP describe prong offsets

XM = X minus; XP = X plus. The prong pair is shifted −15 mm or +15 mm in the plug’s local X direction, relative to an identical housing. YM and YP are the corresponding Y offsets. In the socket-aligned orientation, the correct housing position is socket center − prong offset: XM needs +15 mm in X; XP needs −15 mm. The diagram shows the underside; both prongs are 8 mm wide and 22 mm apart.

The camera image’s left/right direction depends on the view. These labels are geometry variants, not camera modes, training versions or motion commands. Mass and inertia are equalized across variants; the prongs carry the visual identity cue.

  • Slowing the existing policy is not a repair: a 3 mm slew limit reduces performance. Holding its measured TCP at 2 seconds also mostly fails. Changed action dynamics require training under those dynamics.
  • Correcting the final command helps some trajectories: at 2 seconds, a true-target command rescues 18/32 GRU XM episodes and 25/32 yaw episodes, but only 3/32 feedforward XYZ episodes. The latter has usually already reached a state that this simple intervention cannot recover.
  • Approach direction matters: raising while moving sideways solves 29/32 XM and 32/32 of every other variant. The remaining XM starts require housing X around +58 to +64 mm and stall about 63 mm from the target. Lifting in place before traversing removes these failures in the tested set.

Training exploration is much larger than the tolerance

Normalized absolute XYZ actions span 140 ×121 ×35 mm. Thus one action unit corresponds to 70, 60.5 and 17.5 mm. Saved Gaussian standard deviations translate to these physical command scales before clipping and rate limits:

The best GRU checkpoint explores with approximately 17.1 mm X and 13.5 mm Y standard deviation against a 2 mm success threshold. The feedforward XYZ model has almost collapsed Y exploration (0.19 mm) while retaining 27.6 mm X noise. The yaw run retains 22.6° nominal yaw noise against its 2° orientation threshold; yaw motion is separately limited to 3° per step.

Injecting the GRU’s saved noise into the otherwise perfect controller gives 0/512. Reducing that noise tenfold gives 447/512 (87.3%); applying the same normalized noise to bounded 5 mm increments gives 449/512 (87.7%). Both alternatives retain failures. This is direct evidence that the exploration/control scale can prevent precise insertion, even with perfect target information.

Evaluation is already deterministic. The failing baseline uses the actor mean. Lowering the saved standard deviation at evaluation would not fix those means. The noise experiment diagnoses the learning setup; it does not prove how much of the learned failure it caused. The observed deterministic command reversals remain a separate failure.

All noise settings start from matched reset states and images. Later random streams can diverge because successful worlds reset at different times; noise realizations are not claimed to be identical across settings. These are four reset seeds of one checkpoint’s noise parameters, not independent training seeds.

What the evidence supports changing next

  1. Make the approach learnable: train lift → align → insert, using a staged reward or a training curriculum. Dense distance reward alone favors moving toward the final point and does not teach collision clearance. Keep any true target or stage labels in reward/critic/training demonstrations, not the deployed visual actor.
  2. Train precision control at a suitable scale: evaluate incremental TCP commands or coarse/fine control, with exploration reduced during fine insertion. The two successful noise interventions motivate this change; neither has yet been validated as a learned policy.
  3. Keep scoring comparable: retain the physical 2 mm hold check and report orientation separately for a fair yaw ablation, or apply the same orientation criterion to both models. Do not silently relax the benchmark.
  4. Then assess learning duration: 9.23M transitions is much smaller than the legacy 196.6M global budget, but a longer run with destabilizing exploration is not an evidence-based first repair. Use a bounded learning experiment, per-variant curves, multiple training seeds, then extend if competence is improving.

Perception and memory may still limit variant identification; these experiments deliberately bypassed that question. Earlier frozen-feature probes had millimeter-scale localization errors, but that is not proof that the images lack sufficient information. Active-camera superiority and the proposed 80–100% learned targets remain untested hypotheses.

Historical high scores are also not directly comparable: the legacy README marks them superseded because variant-dependent inertia leaked identity through sag. Original diagnosis. No additional learned training or production control/reward change was made during this diagnostic audit.
